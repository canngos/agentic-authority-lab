## P01. Progent: Securing AI Agents with Privilege Control

**Source:** [supplied PDF](<C:/Users/canba/Desktop/university/thesis/reference/authorization-papers/P01_Progent.pdf>), Shi et al., arXiv [2504.11703v3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11703v3), 14 May 2026; 17 pages. Main evidence: pp. 3-12, appendix p. 14. Figures 3-7 visually checked.

**Problem and mechanism.** Progent constrains tool names and arguments with symbolic policies and deterministic enforcement. An LLM proposes initial policies and updates. An SMT check distinguishes narrowing from expansion: an update that permits any previously forbidden call needs approval. This is more expressive than a tool allowlist. Approval, policy generation, and enforcement are separate components.

**What was actually evaluated.** AgentDojo and ASB evaluations plus framework integrations and approval/model ablations. Figure 3, p. 9, reports AgentDojo attack success falling from 39.9% without defense to 1.0%, while benign utility changes from 79.4% to 76.3%. This is one configuration on one benchmark, not a general deployment risk estimate.

**Critical qualifications.** The default empirical configuration automatically approves expansions; the theoretical approval boundary should not be described as human review in those runs. The manual-approval condition assumes informed review and is not a user study measuring approval burden. On p. 14 the authors discuss a benchmark scorer that could count a blocked call as an attack and adjust it. Complete mediation is essential: calls bypassing the protected dispatcher are outside the protection. Correctly enforced but overly broad policies can still permit undesirable actions.

**Overlap with our idea.** Direct and substantial: task-specific tool/argument constraints, runtime enforcement, policy updates, denial explanations, and suggested alternatives already exist. Page 5 describes feedback containing the reason, alternative tools/arguments, and a reminder of the original task. We cannot claim that adding helpful denial feedback is a new mechanism.

**Use in the thesis.** Core related work and enforcement-design reference. Its feedback is a baseline concept to operationalize, rather than a feature to claim as our invention. A controlled feedback study must keep policy and approval behavior fixed. Do not compare its ASR directly with a different task/attack set. [Author repository](https://github.com/sunblaze-ucb/progent).

## P02. Intent-Governed Tool Authorization for AI Agents

**Source:** [supplied PDF](<C:/Users/canba/Desktop/university/thesis/reference/authorization-papers/P02_Intent-Governed.pdf>), Zhu and Wang, arXiv [2606.22916v4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22916v4), 18 September 2026; 34 pages. Main evidence: pp. 7-14, 18-22, 26-27; p. 26 visually checked.

**Problem and mechanism.** A request-bound, short-lived certificate narrows a static OpenPort permission envelope using manifest filtering and payload/effect checks. Routes include denial, clarification, draft, preflight, confirmation, and execution. A draft is not a completed external action. The paper distinguishes the target security contract from bindings implemented in the prototype.

**What was actually evaluated.** Author-constructed deterministic fixtures, a 176-instance runtime-backed synthetic suite, a 34-task real-model study across three models and three repetitions (306 trials), and a small benchmark-family transfer study. The AgentDojo/tau-style cases are locally authored analogues, not full benchmark reproductions. The deterministic certificate conditions and LLM-generated certificate conditions must be distinguished.

**Most consequential result.** In the 306-trial real-model study, 40 of 198 attack-or-ambiguous trials produce unsafe accepted drafts, but none produce completed unsafe effects. Strict benign completion is **0/108**, while **36/108** produce governed artifacts (p. 26). This is not evidence that zero unsafe execution comes with successful autonomous completion. It reflects a review-oriented prototype and should be reported as such.

**Critical qualifications.** Staying inside a static permission envelope does not establish fidelity to the user's specific request. Resource/effect bounds and adapter correctness matter. A post-hoc certificate normalizer re-scores frozen traces without replaying the agent; it reduces unsafe acceptance but also benign artifact yield. There is no matched OpenPort-only real-LLM end-to-end comparison isolating the certificate layer's causal effect (p. 27). The outcome definitions and denominators vary across studies; do not combine their rates.

**Use in the thesis.** Core argument for independently measuring proposal acceptance, executed effects, goal completion, and review/abstention. Reason codes already exist here. Borrow explicit effect boundaries and a trusted task contract, while avoiding LLM certificate generation in the initial experiment.

## P03. MiniScope: Authorizing Agents with Least-Privilege Permissions

**Source:** [supplied PDF](<C:/Users/canba/Desktop/university/thesis/reference/authorization-papers/P03_MiniScope.pdf>), Zhu et al., arXiv [2512.11147v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.11147v2), 6 October 2026; 21 pages. Main evidence: pp. 3-12.

**Mechanism.** Hierarchical roles represent OAuth scopes and API methods, with an optimization procedure selecting sufficient permissions for a plan. Reusable user consent is distinguished from permissions active for the current task. Credentials are isolated, and runtime calls are checked. After exposure to untrusted context, expansion requires approval even when the user previously consented to the role.

**Evaluation.** Synthetic application/persona planning experiments and an adapted AgentDyn setting. The attack evaluation uses simulated approval behavior, including rejection of later expansion requests. Under its selected attacked-task setup, the paper reports a large reduction in attack success with similar attacked-task utility.

**Boundary.** The permission granularity is principally scopes/methods/roles. Page 3 explicitly excludes semantic misuse of an otherwise permitted tool, such as an inappropriate recipient or message body. The residual attacks illustrate this within-scope issue. The evaluation's simulated user is not evidence about actual approval fatigue or human error. A missing clean-utility cell must not be interpreted as a measured zero utility loss.

**Use in the thesis.** Strong evidence that task-active least privilege is already studied. It also makes our resource/argument scope concrete: permission to call `update_order` is different from permission to change order O-17's quantity. The proposed thesis should cite MiniScope for this distinction without claiming that MiniScope's scope is simply ineffective. Do not recreate its optimization and OAuth infrastructure for the initial study.

## P04. Contextual Agent Security: A Policy for Every Purpose (Conseca)

**Source:** [supplied PDF](<C:/Users/canba/Desktop/university/thesis/reference/authorization-papers/P04_Contextual-Agent-Sec.pdf>), Tsai and Bagdasarian, HotOS 2025; arXiv [2501.17070v3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17070v3), 17 April 2025; 10 pages. Main evidence: pp. 3-6.

**Mechanism.** Trusted task/context information is used to generate contextual policies. A deterministic checker applies policies to actions and arguments. The runtime returns decision rationales to the agent, permitting subsequent adaptation. The proof of concept uses a Linux environment, filesystem/email actions, and Gemini 1.5 Pro.

**Evidence.** Twenty handcrafted tasks, five trials, and subjective/manual task construction. On p. 5 the unrestricted condition completes 14/20 tasks; the contextual and permissive-static conditions are around 12/20. The restrictive static policy prohibits writes, while all tasks require writes, producing zero completion. This establishes a small feasibility demonstration, not a general superiority claim over well-engineered static policies.

**Limits.** Policy quality depends on the generator and trusted context. The injection example is illustrative rather than a broad adversarial evaluation. More expressive trajectory-aware policies are discussed as future work.

**Use in the thesis.** Foundational close overlap with the original idea. A small simulated environment is academically plausible, but simply reproducing contextual restrictions and rationales needs explicit positioning as replication or extension. Baselines must be capable of completing legitimate tasks; intentionally disabling all necessary writes would make the comparison uninformative.

## P05. AgentSpec: Customizable Runtime Enforcement for Safe and Reliable LLM Agents

**Source:** [supplied PDF](<C:/Users/canba/Desktop/university/thesis/reference/authorization-papers/P05_AgentSpec.pdf>), Wang, Poskitt, and Sun, ICSE 2026 publication layout; arXiv [2503.18666v3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18666v3), 31 July 2025; 12 pages. Main evidence: pp. 2-10.

**Mechanism.** A DSL expresses triggers, predicates, and enforcement actions, such as stopping, asking for inspection, invoking a function, or asking the LLM to examine itself. The interpreter intercepts agent actions. Predicate implementations and rule correctness determine what is actually guaranteed.

**Evaluation.** Code-execution risks, embodied-agent tasks, and autonomous-driving cases, including 750 code cases, 250 embodied cases, and eight driving scenarios. Both manually written and generated rules are examined. The small driving set and changes in ordinary task completion matter when interpreting headline risk reduction.

**Critical qualifications.** Deterministic dispatch is not equivalent to a deterministic semantic safety decision when a predicate or enforcement step invokes an LLM. Generated rules can be overly restrictive: an example prohibits a whole action class instead of the intended unsafe subset. The measured millisecond parsing/predicate overhead excludes potentially substantial self-examination or human interaction. A ratio between rule count and covered examples is not, by itself, evidence of broad out-of-domain generalization.

**Use in the thesis.** Architecture reference for a pre-execution hook and explicit predicates. For our controlled experiment, simple hand-written predicates and frozen feedback templates make the treatment easier to interpret. A new DSL is unnecessary scope.

## P06. Formal Policy Enforcement for Real-World Agentic Systems (FORGE)

**Source:** [supplied PDF](<C:/Users/canba/Desktop/university/thesis/reference/authorization-papers/P06_Formal-Policy.pdf>), Palumbo et al., arXiv [2602.16708v3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16708v3), 8 May 2026; 36 pages. Older versions use PCAS terminology. Main evidence: pp. 3-14, 34-36; Table 4 visually checked.

**Mechanism.** Aspect weaving instruments tool dispatch and message production. A reference monitor evaluates Datalog policies over an execution dependency graph, identities, arguments, approvals, and external facts. Default denial and deny precedence provide a clear execution boundary. Foreign functions can supply additional facts.

**Formal scope.** The enforcement argument assumes a sound and sufficient environment substrate, complete mediation, and correctly represented policies. A probabilistic semantic foreign function does not become infallible because Datalog consumes its answer. Runtime evaluation guarantees and the decidability of static policy analyses are different; p. 8 restricts the latter analyses because general recursive-Datalog containment is undecidable. Natural-language policy translation remains a separate correctness problem.

**Evaluation.** Exfiltration examples, approval flows, and six selected tau² tasks: three airline and three retail, three models, five trials, totaling 90 per condition. Table 4 reports 52/90 non-instrumented passes versus 88/90 instrumented passes, rounded to 58% and 98%. These are not full-suite tau² results. Appendix examples return structured reasons and explicit recovery instructions. The reported 19% retail and 38% airline time increases include agent continuation; local decision checks are much cheaper.

**Why the feedback gap remains narrower than it first appears.** The comparison changes enforcement and policy presentation together: the non-instrumented agent receives natural-language policy; the instrumented agent relies on runtime enforcement and feedback. It does not isolate a feedback treatment. Page 36 already demonstrates denial followed by required approval and retry. Therefore, neither structured corrective feedback nor a customer-service implementation is new by itself.

**Use in the thesis.** One of the closest papers. Borrow complete mediation, state-dependent predicates, and the distinction between compliance and task reasoning. Keep identical policy information available to all agent conditions and vary only feedback. Do not reproduce the entire weaving/Datalog stack unless integration is itself selected as the contribution.

## P07. ActGov: Governing LLM Agent Actions via Policy-Constrained Validation

**Source:** [supplied PDF](<C:/Users/canba/Desktop/university/thesis/reference/authorization-papers/P07_ActGov.pdf>), Zhang et al., arXiv [2609.24446v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24446v2), 22 September 2026; 15 pages. Main evidence: pp. 2-10.

**Mechanism.** Actions are converted to a finite attribute representation, with deterministic attributes, LLM-derived semantic labels, and composed attributes. Policies are developed offline using traces, formal checks, and human review, then enforced at runtime. It addresses provenance, targets, permission, and execution history.

**Evidence.** AgentDojo/AgentDyn studies and generalization/ablation analyses. The reported protection is strong in some settings, but clean utility decreases can be substantial. For example, a DeepSeek AgentDyn row changes benign utility from .767 to .600 while ASR falls from .086 to .007. Cross-domain rows expose particularly low utility despite very low ASR.

**Critical qualifications.** Deterministic enforcement of extracted attributes is conditional on extraction accuracy. The training/evaluation split is described in task-attack pairs; this does not establish that all underlying task families are disjoint. The policy-development budget and human review differ between main experiments and some ablations. Baselines with zero utility require compatibility investigation before using them as evidence of comparative superiority.

**Use in the thesis.** Important overlap with state-, provenance-, and resource-sensitive authorization. It motivates held-out scenario families and explicit utility controls. Avoid adding learned semantic attributes to the proposed feedback experiment: that would introduce a second source of decision error. No isolated denial-feedback comparison was identified in the inspected evaluation.

## P08. Do Coding Agents Understand Least-Privilege Authorization? (AuthBench)

**Source:** [supplied PDF](<C:/Users/canba/Desktop/university/thesis/reference/authorization-papers/P08_Evolvent-AI.pdf>), **Evolvent AI Research Team** as credited by this PDF; arXiv [2605.14859v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.14859v2), 15 May 2026; 37 pages. Main evidence: pp. 2-10. This is the intended AuthBench paper, despite the filename.

**Question and method.** Can an agent generate permissions that are sufficient for a task yet restrictive enough to exclude malicious activity? The benchmark has 120 terminal tasks (80 standard, 40 sensitive), filesystem read/write/execute policies, and a trace-derived, reviewed reference policy. Policy-generation capability and downstream execution capability are separate roles.

**Evidence.** Full access, a reference least-privilege policy, and generated policies are compared using a fixed execution configuration. Both excessive permission and insufficient permission occur. Iterative sufficient-then-prune policy generation improves some model outcomes, but improvements do not hold uniformly for utility across all models.

**Critical qualifications.** The reference minimum is tied to a demonstrated solution and executor, and need not be the unique minimum across all valid plans. The gold policy itself reduces task completion relative to full access in reported conditions. Runtime implicit permissions are handled separately from task-specific authorization. An authorization model's score must not be confused with the executor model's ability.

**Use in the thesis.** Strong source for the sufficiency-versus-exposure trade-off. Verify that every supposedly recoverable scenario has an authorized witness path; do not label a policy sufficient only because it is narrow. Keep generated-policy quality outside the first study. AuthBench is distinct from AuthorizationBench (P18).

## P09. Hybrid Inspection and Task-Based Access Control in Zero-Trust Agentic AI (CASA)

**Source:** [supplied PDF](<C:/Users/canba/Desktop/university/thesis/reference/authorization-papers/P09_Hybrid-zero-trust-ai.pdf>), El Helou et al., arXiv [2605.02682v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.02682v1), 4 May 2026; 35 pages. Main evidence: pp. 3-9; task-matching prompt p. 35.

**Mechanism.** Five deterministic checks verify integrity across the agent application's tool-message flow: definitions, whether the model requested a call, tool name, parameters, and result fidelity. A semantic layer extracts a task from dialogue and judges task-tool relevance. Definitions received from servers are a trust input; integrity alone does not establish that a model's requested action is authorized.

**Evaluation.** Task-tool pairs originate from enterprise MCP metadata and are expanded into generated conversations using two pipelines. The semantic evaluation classifies conversations at the first tool call; it does not measure end-to-end task recovery. The multi-agent generator uses simulated participants/tools. Test and validation sets are separated by MCP server.

**Limits.** The two semantic stages can compound errors: task extraction can omit or invent intent; matching can over- or under-authorize. Reported multi-turn false-positive rates are material, even when aggregate F1 is high. Matching is largely about whether a tool helps the task, rather than whether particular arguments and effects satisfy an exact task contract. Full-history extraction brings a separate trust challenge.

**Use in the thesis.** Helps distinguish transport integrity, tool relevance, and exact authority. Our experiment should not treat these as interchangeable. A full MCP interception and OAuth architecture would add integration work without isolating the proposed feedback question.

## P10. Delegated Authorization for Agents Constrained to Semantic Task-to-Scope Matching (ASTRA)

**Source:** [supplied PDF](<C:/Users/canba/Desktop/university/thesis/reference/authorization-papers/P10_Delegated-Authorization.pdf>), El Helou et al., arXiv [2510.26702v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.26702v1), 30 October 2025; 18 pages. Main evidence: pp. 3-8.

**Mechanism.** A trusted proxy captures the original task for an authorization service. Semantic matching compares task intent with requested scopes/tools, using either embeddings or LLM reasoning. The architecture proposes task-limited delegation; the main empirical question is matching accuracy.

**Evaluation.** Synthetic tasks based on 352 tools from 12 MCP servers, single- and multiple-tool requests, plus a Toucan-derived evaluation. Relevant and irrelevant tools are sampled under a specified distribution. Splits consider tool/server separation. Multi-tool performance and accumulated false denials degrade relative to the simpler single-tool setting.

**Limits.** Classification accuracy is not an end-to-end completion or security rate. The task generation and negative sampling shape the difficulty. Determining that a tool is relevant does not validate its recipient, record identifier, quantity, or downstream side effects. Architectural discussion of automatic revocation should not be mistaken for an experimentally validated stateful revocation system.

**Use in the thesis.** Related authorization infrastructure and evidence that underscoping matters. Together with P09, this argues for fixing a trusted, explicit task contract when studying recovery, so feedback effects are not confounded with task interpretation errors.

## P11. The Verifier Tax: Horizon Dependent Safety-Success Tradeoffs in Tool Using LLM Agents

**Source:** [supplied PDF](<C:/Users/canba/Desktop/university/thesis/reference/authorization-papers/P11_Verifier-Tax.pdf>), Sah, Srivastava, and Sah, arXiv [2603.19328v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19328v1), 18 March 2026; 14 pages. Main evidence: pp. 3-9; Tables 5-6 visually checked.

**Question and method.** The study compares a tool-calling agent with planner-actor-verifier configurations on tau-bench retail/airline. It separates task success, safe success, and successful-but-violating trajectories. There are 165 tasks, two subject models, three configurations, and three repetitions: 2,970 runs. It examines recovery following interventions and additional calls/tokens.

**Important protocol detail.** In the main experiment, after three verifier rejections the last proposal is automatically accepted. A fail-closed alternative is analyzed retrospectively rather than run as a fully independent experiment. This makes the study materially different from a reference monitor that never executes a denied call. The models also have different action caps, and the LLM user simulator introduces stochastic variation.

**Evidence and limits.** Verification adds substantial calls and estimated tokens, and interventions often do not lead to success. However, recovery denominators differ between safety and broader policy interventions. Some safety cells are extremely small: the discussion reports 0/3 retail safety recoveries for one relevant model setting. This does not support a universal numerical recovery-failure rate. The audit uses deterministic checks plus an LLM judge; independent label accuracy is not fully established. Claims that more retries would not help go beyond an actual controlled comparison of larger retry budgets.

**Use in the thesis.** Strong motivation to separate goal completion from compliance and to measure recovery costs. Do not use its headline recovery rates as a power estimate without understanding denominators. Our study should never execute a blocked proposal after exhausting retries, and should hold the action budget fixed across feedback arms.

## P12. CRITICTOOL: Evaluating Self-Critique Capabilities of Large Language Models in Tool-Calling Error Scenarios

**Source:** [supplied PDF](<C:/Users/canba/Desktop/university/thesis/reference/authorization-papers/P12_CriticTool.pdf>), Huang et al., arXiv [2506.13977v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.13977v1), 11 June 2025; 32 pages. Main evidence: pp. 3-8.

**Question and material.** The benchmark tests whether a model can identify and correct errors after a supplied erroneous tool-use prefix. It covers tool selection, nonexistent tools, parameter keys, parameter values, and environment errors. There are 1,490 base and 1,250 evolved examples; tool material derives from BFCL/TEval. Error creation and responses involve model generation, with filtering and a mix of cached, actual, and simulated API outputs.

**Evaluation distinction.** The model critiques or corrects a specified failure and can retry, skip, or finish. The aggregate score combines reflection, correction, retry, and skip/finish components with different weights. It is not the percentage of complete business workflows solved correctly. Permission-denied errors appear within a broad environment-error category.

**Limits.** A permission denial and a temporary API outage require different responses. Repairing syntax or choosing a plausible next call does not establish that the action remains authorized. Reference corrections can also be narrower than the set of all valid recovery paths. Filtering generated examples can change the distribution of errors.

**Use in the thesis.** Useful precedent for a shared pre-failure trace and controlled continuation. Borrow error taxonomy and critique analysis, but ground our outcomes in environment state and policy rather than a single expected next action. Do not claim that recovery from tool errors has not been studied.

## P13. When Tools Fail: Benchmarking Dynamic Replanning and Anomaly Recovery in LLM Agents (ToolMaze)

**Source:** [supplied PDF](<C:/Users/canba/Desktop/university/thesis/reference/authorization-papers/P13_When-Tools-Fail.pdf>), Zhu et al., arXiv [2606.05806v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05806v1), 4 June 2026; 38 pages. Main evidence: pp. 3-9; perturbation rules and metric definitions on p. 5 visually checked.

**Mechanism and material.** ToolMaze constructs dependency graphs with different alternative-path structures. Four hundred base tasks expand to 2,000 instances across a clean condition and four combinations of explicit/implicit and transient/permanent faults. The toolkit contains 270 tools. A deterministic fault injector and simulated tool execution create controlled disturbances. Nine models are evaluated under a fixed step limit.

**What it measures.** Task success, recovery after perturbation, and replanning cost. Fault activation can target the first tool selected from an alternative group, ensuring that simply choosing another initial path does not evade exposure. Valid paths are enumerated from the task construction.

**Critical interpretation.** Its success criterion can include graceful termination when the topology has no remaining path (p. 5). Therefore its task-success rate is not always strict original-goal completion. Transient faults make retry sensible; permanent faults require an alternative or stopping. A failure-aware prompt supplies both awareness and strategies, so its effect is not a pure message-format comparison. Graph-grounded synthetic tasks do not establish realistic business authority semantics.

**Use in the thesis.** The most useful generic-recovery source for feasibility strata. Our difference must be persistent task-specific authorization, not merely introducing retries or alternative paths. An illegal path must remain illegal across retries. Separate lawful goal completion and justified stopping instead of combining both into one success score. Full ToolMaze integration is unnecessary for a single order-management study.

## P14. Causality Laundering: Denial-Feedback Leakage in Tool-Calling LLM Agents

**Source:** [supplied PDF](<C:/Users/canba/Desktop/university/thesis/reference/authorization-papers/P14_Causality-Laundering.pdf>), Chinaei, arXiv [2604.04035v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04035v1), 5 April 2026; 24 pages. Main evidence: pp. 5-8, 14-16, 19-20.

**Problem and mechanism.** A blocked action can still leak information through its denial, and subsequent actions can reuse that information. The proposed authorization reference monitor tracks provenance and denied actions in an execution graph and combines hard bounds, provenance checks, schema validation, and approval.

**Evidence.** Three constructed differential scenarios compare a flat check with graph-sensitive rules: denial-derived information leakage, transitive taint, and mixed-trust fields. Local performance measurements use small graphs and repeated warm runs. This is a mechanism demonstration, not an extensive end-to-end LLM attack-success evaluation.

**Limits.** An edge from a denial to the next action is an operational approximation of dependency, not causal identification. It could implicate harmless follow-up actions or miss delayed use. Field trust classifications and threat coverage are design assumptions. The examples establish that some denials can disclose too much, not that all explanatory feedback is unsafe.

**Use in the thesis.** Treat feedback itself as an information release. Supply task-visible constraints and bounded repair guidance, not hidden record lists, secret values, or confirmation of a confidential record's existence. Include canary checks in development. Avoid a separate provenance-system thesis unless this becomes the chosen contribution. A fuller feedback-disclosure experiment would require explicit threat models and additional literature.

## P15. ToolMisuseBench: An Offline Deterministic Benchmark for Tool Misuse and Recovery in Agentic Systems

**Source:** [supplied PDF](<C:/Users/canba/Desktop/university/thesis/reference/authorization-papers/P15_ToolMisuseBench.pdf>), Sigdel and Baral, arXiv [2604.01508v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01508v1), 2 April 2026; four pages. Main evidence: entire short paper, especially pp. 2-4; results tables on p. 3 visually checked.

**Material.** Deterministic CRUD, retrieval, file, and scheduling environments with schema drift, timeout, rate-limit, authorization, and adversarial-error faults. The paper describes 5,000/800/1,000 training/development/test instances and budgeted execution.

**What the supplied paper actually tested.** Three deterministic reference agents: heuristic, schema-repair, and policy-aware. These are not three evaluated LLM agents. All obtain .25 overall success; all have zero success on the released authorization and rate-limit subsets. These results show limitations of those baselines on that release, not that modern LLM agents universally fail after denial. Solvability under the available authority must be audited before interpreting a zero score as poor recovery.

**Version warning from the web check.** The author's current [dataset card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/sigdelakshey/ToolMisuseBench) describes a **v0.2** release with typed recoverability and 6,000/900/1,200 instances. Its preview includes `transient_denial`, and it lists LLM experiment artifacts. This differs from the downloaded paper. The [repository README](https://github.com/akgitrepos/toolmisusebench) still presents v0.1 reproduction commands. Neither later experiment results nor fault implementations were independently reproduced here. Do not apply the PDF's criticism or counts indiscriminately to the newer release.

**Use in the thesis.** A potentially reusable fault/trace harness and a close overlap check. Before adoption, reconcile paper, dataset, code commit, denial persistence, and success semantics. A transient denial is not the same as a persistent authorization boundary. The thesis cannot claim to be the first recovery benchmark with an authorization-error category.

## P16. When Tool Outputs Become Commands: Separating Action Induction from Runtime Authorization in Tool-Augmented LLM Agents (SARA)

**Source:** [supplied PDF](<C:/Users/canba/Desktop/university/thesis/reference/authorization-papers/P16_When-Tool-Outputs-Become-Commands.pdf>), Guo et al., arXiv [2608.27146v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27146v1), 27 August 2026; 17 pages. Main evidence: pp. 4-10, 12-13.

**Mechanism.** SARA separates data supplied by tools from authority to induce actions. It tracks a user-rooted authorization context, action-inducing evidence, and successfully authorized execution history. It classifies parameters by role and checks their relationship to the task. Prior appearance in the conversation does not automatically make a value authorized. Review activates on exposure; other actions can follow a faster path.

**Evidence.** AgentDojo and AgentDyn studies report large attack reductions, accompanied by utility and token-cost changes. The paper's task/attack counts differ from the original AgentDojo release and other defenses, so raw ASR comparisons across papers are invalid. Its component ablations concern the authorization architecture, not an isolated feedback comparison.

**Limits.** Semantic guards use LLM judgments. The authors explicitly do not claim a formal security guarantee (p. 13). Page 8 already returns denial reasons for replanning. Strong attack protection does not imply unchanged benign completion; several clean-utility rows decline. Semantic authorization is broader than deterministic argument equality and has different error sources.

**Use in the thesis.** Core evidence against claiming that dynamic identifiers, parameter roles, provenance, or reason-based replanning are new individually. Reuse the principle that a tool-returned identifier becomes authorized only through a trusted relationship to the task. Keep our enforcement deterministic and narrow enough to validate independently.

## P17. AgentBoundary: Counterfactual Evaluation of Safety in Tool-Using LLM Agents

**Source:** [supplied PDF](<C:/Users/canba/Desktop/university/thesis/reference/authorization-papers/P17_Agent-Boundary.pdf>), Yang et al., arXiv [2609.33658v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33658v1), 27 September 2026; 31 pages. Main evidence: pp. 3-9. This is AgentBoundary, distinct from the separate AgentBound execution-boundary paper in the earlier download guide.

**Question and design.** Can an agent discriminate harmful actions from legitimate tasks that merely contain suspicious cues? Matched families include harmful-source, covert-malicious, easy-benign, and hard-benign variants. The evaluation uses 1,000 families/4,000 tasks and 17 configurations. A larger construction/validation pool is separate from that evaluation set.

**Evidence.** The counterfactual structure reveals both missed harm and over-refusal. A trained thought-alignment intervention improves reported benign completion and some safety outcomes. These are model-behavior improvements, not deterministic authority enforcement.

**Limits.** Tool execution and grading include emulation and LLM judgments, with human validation. Reported agreement allowing a one-point score difference is not exact binary-label agreement. The paper's broad conceptual definition and operational scoring of unsafe proposals/effects need careful mapping if borrowing metrics. Variants within a family are statistically dependent.

**Use in the thesis.** Borrow matched benign controls and family-level splits. A denial experiment should include a legitimate task with similar surface cues, so excessive stopping is visible. We do not need to train an aligner. Keep a whole task family together when tuning, holding out, and estimating uncertainty.

## P18. Act on Your Behalf, Beyond Your Authorization: Benchmarking LLM Agent Authorization Alignment (AuthorizationBench)

**Source:** [supplied PDF](<C:/Users/canba/Desktop/university/thesis/reference/authorization-papers/P18_Act-On-Your-Behalf.pdf>), Zhang, Li, Huang, and Li; 25-page author-hosted preprint. [Author PDF](https://www.zhipingzhang.com/_files/ugd/3840b5_415101659c9f406d888aa5fce66889d5.pdf). The supplied first page contains no arXiv identifier; none is invented here. Main evidence: pp. 3-9.

**Question and material.** Agents can exceed authority while trying to help, without a malicious prompt. AuthorizationBench develops task contracts from 44 seed cases, expands to 286 tasks, and uses 38 deterministic service templates across ten domains. It separately specifies instructions, acceptable completion, and out-of-scope actions. Both legitimate and unauthorized paths remain executable, so behavior can be observed without a blocking layer.

**Evidence.** Four models, two effort settings, and three harnesses yield 24 configurations, with additional repeated-run checks. The reported overall overstepping rate is 19.2% on this constructed benchmark. Matched benign-pressure and malicious-pressure cases show that ordinary pressure can induce substantial overreach. Consultation is associated with lower overstepping; the reported association does not identify consultation's causal effect.

**Limits.** The set of explicitly labeled out-of-scope behaviors is not a proof of exhaustive coverage. The oracle user is simulated and does not grant authority merely because the agent asks. Most main cells have one run, while repeatability is assessed on a subset. The 19.2% figure is not the probability that an agent exceeds authority in arbitrary real use.

**Use in the thesis.** Strong justification for including benign wrong-target/helpful-extra-work cases as well as injections. Preserve separate goal and authority predicates. An unguarded observation benchmark and an always-enforced recovery study answer different questions; do not report zero executed overreach under our guard as improved intrinsic alignment.

## P19. AgentDojo: A Dynamic Environment to Evaluate Prompt Injection Attacks and Defenses for LLM Agents

**Source:** [supplied PDF](<C:/Users/canba/Desktop/university/thesis/reference/authorization-papers/P19_AgentDojo.pdf>), Debenedetti et al., NeurIPS 2024 Datasets and Benchmarks; arXiv [2406.13352v3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.13352v3), 24 November 2024; 26 pages. Main evidence: pp. 3-9. [Author repository](https://github.com/ethz-spylab/agentdojo).

**Material and method.** Stateful Python environments represent workspace, banking, travel, and Slack workflows. The supplied version has 97 user tasks, 27 attacker goals, and 629 task-attack pairs. Tools and outputs expose lower-trust material to the agent. Task and attacker success are checked using environment state and, for some tasks, response content.

**Findings relevant to authorization.** Tool filtering helps but cannot solve attacks that use a tool legitimately required by the user's task. The paper notes this shared-tool issue for a meaningful subset of cases (p. 9). Task/resource/argument constraints are therefore more informative than merely shrinking the visible tool list.

**Limits.** This is a prompt-injection benchmark, not a comprehensive authorization-denial recovery benchmark. Its selected attack model, task set, tools, and success checks define its coverage. The original utility-under-attack definition excludes successful adversarial side effects; later papers can use different conventions. Versions differ across follow-up studies.

**Use in the thesis.** Strong reusable ideas: resettable state, explicit attacker goals, deterministic effect checking, and separate clean/attacked runs. Either extend a pinned version or implement a small transparent environment using these principles. Adapting task ideas is not a benchmark reproduction. For the primary recovery question, a broad adversarial benchmark would add variance and scope.

## P20. tau-bench: A Benchmark for Tool-Agent-User Interaction in Real-World Domains

**Source:** [supplied PDF](<C:/Users/canba/Desktop/university/thesis/reference/authorization-papers/P20_t-Bench.pdf>), Yao, Shinn, Razavi, and Narasimhan, arXiv [2406.12045v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045v1), 17 June 2024; 50 pages. Main evidence: pp. 3-9; Figure 2 and reward definition on p. 4 visually checked.

**Material.** JSON databases, Python tools, natural-language domain policies, and simulated users. The original release includes 115 retail and 50 airline tasks. It supplies an especially relevant template for order-state changes and policy-sensitive workflows.

**Measurement.** Reward checks the final database and required response outputs. Page 4 explicitly warns that a correct final state can still be reached without required user confirmation. Reward is therefore not sufficient to establish policy compliance. The paper introduces pass^k to measure success across all k trials; it is different from pass@k, which rewards at least one success.

**Limits.** The user simulator adds model-specific and stochastic influences. Original task assumptions favor a specific reference outcome and need auditing when multiple valid outcomes exist. Published older-model scores should not be used to predict present-model ability. The benchmark does not independently establish that every intermediate action was authorized.

**Artifact status check.** The [official repository](https://github.com/sierra-research/tau-bench) now warns that its tasks are outdated and points to the successor repository. This does not invalidate the paper; it means implementation must record the exact code/data version and avoid silently mixing tau, tau², and later tasks.

**Use in the thesis.** Best business-workflow inspiration in the supplied corpus. Use an independent authority/trajectory oracle alongside goal-state checks. A deterministic task script is preferable to a second LLM user for the first causal feedback experiment. A small clearly labeled adaptation can later check transfer beyond authored tasks.

## Corrections and evidence boundaries to carry forward

- P08 is AuthBench; P18 is AuthorizationBench; P17 is AgentBoundary. These are different benchmarks.
- P06's reviewed version is FORGE, not the older PCAS formulation.
- P01 already provides denial reasons and alternatives; P04 returns rationales; P06 provides structured corrective instructions; P16 supports reason-based replanning.
- P02's zero unsafe effects must be accompanied by its zero strict benign completions in the real-model study.
- P11's main verifier protocol eventually allows the rejected proposal; it is not equivalent to persistent fail-closed enforcement.
- P15's supplied results are heuristic-agent results. Its current dataset has changed; code/data/paper versions require reconciliation.
- P13 may count appropriate termination as success; P20 reward may overlook a policy violation. Neither metric can be copied without adaptation.
- Stronger guarantees always depend on a stated boundary: faithful policy, trustworthy facts, correctly implemented tools, and mediation of all relevant effects.
- The 20 papers establish close prior art and useful methods. They do not establish an exhaustive novelty claim or industrial deployment effectiveness.
